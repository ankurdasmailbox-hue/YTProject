Full Script

Look closely at the blue oceans and continental shelves of modern Earth. Now strip them away. Strip away the atmosphere, the soil, and the granite crust. Four and a half billion years ago, there was no ground to stand on—only a roiling ocean of liquid rock. The planetary chronicle begins with fundamental formation and gravitational accretion. The Hadean is the first and oldest of the four geologic eons of Earth's history, starting with the planet's formation about 4.6 Ga, and ending 4.031 Ga, the age of the oldest known intact rock formations on Earth as recognized by the International Commission on Stratigraphy. The interplanetary collision that created the Moon occurred early in this eon. A defining cataclysm of Earth's infancy occurred through a high-velocity planetary collision. The giant-impact hypothesis, sometimes called the Theia Impact, is an astrogeology hypothesis for the formation of the Moon first proposed in 1946 by Canadian geologist Reginald Daly. The hypothesis suggests that the Proto-Earth collided with a Mars-sized co-orbital protoplanet likely from the L4 or L5 Lagrange points of the Earth's orbit approximately 4.5 billion years ago in the early Hadean eon (about 20 to 100 million years after the Solar System formed), and some of the ejected debris from the impact event later re-accreted to form the Moon. The resulting thermal release liquefied the planet's exterior into a planetary magma ocean. Magma oceans are vast fields of surface magma that exist during periods of a planet's or some natural satellite's accretion when the celestial body is completely or partly molten. Critical mineralogical archives demonstrate that the cooling process was surprisingly rapid. The Jack Hills are a range of hills in Mid West Western Australia. They are best known as the source of the oldest material of terrestrial origin found to date: Hadean zircons that formed around 4.404 billion years ago. Throughout this era, planetary surface evolution was shaped by intense impact flux. The Late Heavy Bombardment (LHB), or lunar cataclysm, is a hypothesized astronomical event thought to have occurred approximately 4.1 to 3.8 billion years (Ga) ago, at a time corresponding to the Neohadean and Eoarchean eras on Earth. According to the hypothesis, during this interval, a disproportionately large number of asteroids and comets collided into the terrestrial planets and their natural satellites in the inner Solar System, including Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars. The Hadean was not merely an inferno; it was the planetary crucible where the physical and chemical foundations of our world were forged. From magma oceans to the first droplets of condensed rain, the architecture of life had already begun.
